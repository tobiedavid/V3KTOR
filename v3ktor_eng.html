Most of the signal you receive in a day is about other people: their stories, their agendas, their content. Without a fixed reference point you drift with it, and after a while you mistake the noise for your own voice. V3KTOR builds the reference point. Measured, not narrated.
One word at a time. Positive, tap right. Negative, tap left. Your thumb answers before your story does, and the time it takes is the measurement.
Every word carries the crowd's values for how positive, how intense and how dominant it feels (NRC VAD Lexicon, National Research Council Canada). Your answer is set against them. Where you agree is the shared floor. Where you part ways is you.
The engine adapts. It serves more from what you react to quickly, spaces its follow-ups so one answer can't pull the next, places a turning point after a dark run, and remembers which words it chose for you so they count less than the ones you met cold.
The timing is corrected for what is known to distort it on a phone: how you hold it, the length of the word, the pull of the previous answer, fatigue within the session, a foggy day. What remains is read through two standard models of decision-making: how clearly the evidence came, how much you demanded before committing, how far you lean toward yes.
Before each session you say how you feel, in four taps and an optional line of context. The test records what your thumb does. The gap between the two is kept, because it has a shape.
One session is a reading. Each one after it raises the resolution. Words met again confirm or contradict. Contexts that flip a word show themselves. Foggy sessions count less toward speed; what you answered on them counts in full. Sittings on different days in different states count most.
The map checks itself. Every ten sittings close a block; from twenty on, each new block is checked against the one before it, and the map tells you in one line how much to trust it. Before that, a provisional figure stands in. The thresholds can't be lowered. A map that takes months to confirm is one you have watched form.
What comes out: what you affirm, what you refuse, the exceptions that hold, and a file that carries its own confidence. Paste it into Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini for a reading, or show it to an AI so it stops guessing who it is talking to. The map is yours. Nothing here is built for a machine to steer by.
Not a clinical tool, and not a copy of anyone. None of the parts are new: the word values, the frequency norms, the reaction-time paradigm, the decision models were all published, and are used here as published. What is new is the assembly, for one person, over months, on a phone: a map of what you answer before you explain, the layer under the story you tell others and yourself, measured rather than narrated. No one has tested the whole. That test is the one you run by using it.

MOTOR CHECK
Eight arrows before the first word, tapped on the side they point to, measure each thumb's plain movement time. Off, the yes/no speed difference can't be split into hand and decision.
FIXATION DOT
The pause before each word varies in length so the thumb can't fall into a rhythm. On, a small dim dot marks the centre during it; off, the screen is blank.
PRESSURE MODE
The engine chases what you react to, with no spacing and a looser cap (its queue can hold up to 60% of the session, against 30%) — the way the first version did. Same words, same timing. Sessions are tagged PRESSURE, kept in the log, and left out of the baseline and calibration.
